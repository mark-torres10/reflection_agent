# Application questions

1. Can you describe a project where you tuned, fine-tuned, or architected an LLM system to achieve a very specific tone, personality, or behavioral style? What approach did you take, and how did you evaluate the quality of outputs?

I built an AI agent that drafts blog posts in a specific person’s voice (https://www.loom.com/share/f9bf8a4491c7465bb780e58fbb0104d7). The approach starts with the human: we collected a small set of their best posts, interview them on what “sounds like me” vs. what feels off, and turn that into a short style guide (tone, pacing, phrases they use or avoid, how they open and close pieces). That guide, plus a few real examples embedded in the prompts, becomes the core of the system. We set this up in steps so that the model learns over time what "sounds like" the person. Along the way, we ask for human feedback to make sure that the AI exactly matches criteria from the human facilitators, even developing our own models to grade outputs like the human experts would.

Quality is judged by the person whose voice it is, not by the model. We used a simple rubric: does this sound like them, is it specific rather than filler, and is it factually acceptable? We compared drafts side-by-side with real posts, captured specific failure modes ("they'd never say those words", "sounds too much like AI", "the AI made up facts"), and revised prompts and examples until those patterns disappeared.

2. How would you design an AI system that maintains emotional continuity and personalized “memory” across multi-day user conversations while keeping responses natural and non-generic?

I would treat each multi-day journey as its own thread with a small set of stored notes—not the full chat log. At the end of each reflection, the system would capture a short summary: themes the user named, emotional tone, open questions, and anything they asked to revisit. Separately, we would keep a few stable “user facets” (e.g., recurring goals or language they use) that persist across journeys. When the user returns, prompts would pull only what matters for that moment—enough to show continuity (“last time you were sitting with X”) without sounding like a recap bot or a dossier.

Facilitator methodology would live in the prompt structure itself: tone rules, pacing, what good reflection looks like, and when not to push deeper. Memory would feed that structure, not replace it. For the MVP I would start with curated summaries plus facilitator-reviewed examples of strong vs. generic responses, and iterate with your team using a simple rubric (specific, calm, emotionally accurate) until outputs consistently earn “that understood me.” Heavier approaches—fine-tuning or richer personalization—would come only once we know what to optimize for.

## Draft answer for Question 2

When someone comes back on day two or day five, it should feel like the same calm guide—not a stranger who read their entire history. I would not save every message. After each reflection, the system writes a short note in plain language: what mattered to them, the emotional tone, and anything they wanted to pick up later. Across journeys we keep only a handful of lasting details (recurring themes, how they like to be spoken to). The next conversation uses just enough of that to feel personal—“you were sitting with this last time”—without reciting their story back to them.

Your facilitators’ way of working stays in charge: pacing, when to go deeper vs. hold space, and what good reflection sounds like. Those rules shape every reply; the notes support them, they do not replace them.
